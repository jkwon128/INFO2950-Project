Research Question¶

Inflation is one of the most closely followed numbers in the U.S. economy, and it is widely assumed to matter for the stock market. The usual reasoning runs through valuation. When inflation rises, the Federal Reserve tends to raise short-term interest rates. A higher interest rate means investors discount future corporate earnings more heavily, so they pay less today for the same stream of future profits. Rising input costs can squeeze profit margins at the same time. Both arguments point the same way: months with higher inflation should tend to have weaker stock returns.

The reasoning is not airtight. Inflation often rises because demand is strong, and strong demand is good for corporate earnings. So the sign of the relationship is genuinely an empirical question rather than something we can settle by argument.

We study that question using monthly U.S. data. Our outcome is the monthly price return on the S&P 500. Our main explanatory variable is the year-over-year inflation rate computed from the Consumer Price Index. We ask:

How does U.S. inflation relate to monthly S&P 500 price returns between January 2017 and September 2026? In particular, do months with higher year-over-year headline CPI inflation show lower returns, does the same pattern hold when food and energy are excluded, and how do consumers' expectations of future inflation compare with inflation that actually occurred?

Our aim is to describe how one widely reported indicator lines up with stock market performance, and to be honest about how much of the month-to-month movement in returns it fails to explain.

A note on scope. Our analysis is observational, and we compare inflation and returns measured over the same calendar month. We therefore describe associations. We do not claim that inflation causes returns to change, and we do not propose a trading strategy.

Data Collection and Cleaning¶

Collection¶

We combine data from two meaningfully distinct public sources. All files were downloaded as .csv and are stored unmodified in data/raw/; every transformation below begins from those files.

Source 1 — Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED), Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. We downloaded three series directly from their FRED series pages:

Series ID Measures Frequency Produced by
SP500 S&P 500 closing index level (price only, no dividends) Daily S&P Dow Jones Indices LLC
CPIAUCNS CPI for All Urban Consumers, All Items, not seasonally adjusted Monthly U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics
CPILFENS CPI for All Urban Consumers, All Items Less Food and Energy, not seasonally adjusted Monthly U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics

Source 2 — University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers, distributed through FRED:

Series ID Measures Frequency Produced by
MICH Median expected price change over the next 12 months Monthly University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers

These are distinct sources rather than one dataset under different names. The S&P 500 series is market transaction data from a private index provider. The two CPI series come from a government price survey. MICH is a household opinion survey run by a university. What lets us put them together is the calendar month, and the combination is the point: no single one of these files can tell us how inflation and stock returns move relative to each other.

Two choices in the selection are worth explaining. We use the not-seasonally-adjusted CPI because we compute year-over-year changes, and a twelve-month change already removes the seasonal pattern that seasonal adjustment exists to remove. We use the price-only S&P 500 series because that is what FRED publishes; the consequences of leaving out dividends are discussed under Data Limitations.

Four things stand out, and each one shapes a later step.

The S&P 500 file has 2,599 daily rows running from 14 October 2016 to 30 September 2026, and 96 of them have no closing price. These are market holidays: FRED keeps the calendar row but there is no price because the exchange was shut. They are not errors, and we drop them rather than filling them in. Inventing a price for a day with no trading would mean fabricating data.

Both CPI files have 141 monthly rows from January 2015 to September 2026, and both are missing exactly one value. Step 2b identifies which month, because the answer turns out to matter.

The MICH file has 119 monthly rows from October 2016 to August 2026 and no missing values. It starts later and ends a month earlier than the CPI series, so expected inflation will be available for fewer months than realized inflation.

Finally, the four files are at different frequencies, one daily and three monthly, so they cannot be merged until the S&P 500 series has been reduced to one row per month.

Step 3 — Standardize names, dates and calendar columns¶

We give the columns short descriptive names and convert the date columns to datetime objects. We then create a Year column and a Month column for each file, which is how we will line the four series up with each other.

We also create a single month_index column, defined as Year * 12 + Month. This is simply a running count of months, so that the month before any given month is always one lower and the same month a year earlier is always twelve lower. Having that number makes the comparisons in Steps 4 and 5 straightforward, because a shift in time becomes ordinary arithmetic followed by a merge.

Note that we do not need to convert the value columns to numbers. The blank entries in the raw files are read as missing values automatically, which the inspection in Step 2 already confirmed.

Step 4 — Reduce daily prices to monthly returns¶

Our unit of observation is the calendar month, but the S&P 500 series is daily, so we reduce it to one row per month.

We take the closing price on the last trading day of each month rather than the monthly average. A monthly average blends prices from across the month and does not correspond to a return any investor could have earned. Using month-end closes lets us define the monthly return as the percent change from one month-end price to the next, which is the standard definition of a monthly price return.

To find the last trading day we group by year and month and take the latest date in each group, then merge that date back onto the daily data to recover its closing price. To compute the return we make a second copy of the monthly table with month_index increased by one, which lines each month up against the month before it.

Step 5 — Compute year-over-year inflation¶

Inflation is not the level of the CPI but its rate of change. Following our mentor's advice in Phase I we use the year-over-year change, which is the percent change in the index from the same month one year earlier. A twelve-month change is less sensitive to short-term monthly movements than a month-over-month change, and it is the form in which inflation is normally reported.

For month $t$ the year-over-year rate in percent is

$$\text{inflation}_t = \frac{\text{CPI}_t - \text{CPI}_{t-12}}{\text{CPI}_{t-12}} \times 100$$

We build this with the same approach used for returns in Step 4: make a copy of the CPI table with month_index increased by twelve and merge it back, so that each month sits alongside its own base month. Doing it this way keeps the alignment visible, and a month with no data produces a missing rate rather than being silently skipped.

In [6]:
# Build a year-over-year percent change for one CPI series
def add_year_over_year(frame, level_column, new_column):
    """Return a monthly table with a year-over-year percent change column.

    frame        : a monthly CPI frame with month_index, Year, Month and a level column
    level_column : name of the CPI level column
    new_column   : name to give the new percent change column
    """
    result = frame[["month_index", "Year", "Month", level_column]].copy()

    # Copy the same data labelled twelve months later, so each month meets its base year
    base = result[["month_index", level_column]].copy()
    base["month_index"] = base["month_index"] + 12
    base = base.rename(columns={level_column: "base_value"})

    result = result.merge(base, on="month_index", how="left")
    result[new_column] = (result[level_column] - result["base_value"]) / result["base_value"] * 100
    return result.drop(columns="base_value")

The dataset has 116 rows, one for each calendar month from January 2017 to September 2026 except October 2025. Each row represents one month of the U.S. economy and the U.S. stock market.

Why was this dataset created?¶

We built it to examine whether months of higher U.S. inflation coincide with weaker S&P 500 returns. No public file contains both inflation and monthly equity returns on a shared monthly key, so the dataset exists in order to place a government price survey, a market index and a household survey on the same monthly footing. It was assembled for an INFO 2950 course project at Cornell University and is intended to support descriptive comparison and simple regression.

How did you create this dataset? What tools, procedures and data sources did you use?¶

We downloaded four .csv files: SP500, CPIAUCNS and CPILFENS from FRED, and the University of Michigan MICH series distributed through FRED. We combined them in this notebook using Python and pandas. The procedure is the seven steps above: inspect the files, standardize names and calendar columns, reduce daily prices to month-end closes and compute monthly returns, compute year-over-year inflation, check each monthly series for duplicates, and merge on the month counter. Figures use matplotlib and seaborn, and the regressions use LinearRegression from scikit-learn. We collected no data ourselves, and all four sources are free to access.

Who funded the creation of the dataset?¶

The underlying series come from publicly funded or publicly accessible institutions. The Bureau of Labor Statistics, a federal agency, produces both CPI series. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis distributes them through FRED along with the S&P 500 series. S&P Dow Jones Indices LLC produces the index itself. The University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers produces the expectations series. We assembled the merged dataset with no external funding.

What processes might have influenced what data was observed and recorded, and what was not?¶

Several decisions, none of them ours, shape what this dataset contains.

What counts as inflation is itself a methodological choice. The CPI tracks the price of a fixed basket of goods and services for urban consumers, so it reflects BLS decisions about basket composition, geographic coverage, substitution and quality adjustment. Urban consumers are about 93% of the U.S. population, which means rural price experience is underweighted.

October 2025 is missing because of a political event rather than a measurement failure. The government shutdown suspended BLS collection and that month was never published. Our data therefore have a gap created by how the statistical system is funded and governed.

The S&P 500 series is a price index, so dividends are invisible to us. Total investor returns, which include reinvested dividends, are higher than what we observe, historically by roughly two percentage points a year. The index also covers only large publicly traded U.S. companies, so private firms, small caps and non-U.S. markets are outside its scope by construction.

Aggregating to months conceals timing within the month. A month-end-to-month-end return cannot distinguish a steady climb from a crash followed by a recovery.

Finally, expectations are self-reported. MICH records what survey respondents say they expect, which may reflect general feeling about the economy as much as a considered forecast, and it is reported as a median across respondents rather than a consensus view.

What preprocessing was done, and how did the data come to be in the form that you are using?¶

  1. Four raw .csv files were read from data/raw/ without modification.
  2. Shapes, first rows and missing-value counts were checked for each file, and the one missing CPI month was identified as October 2025.
  3. Columns were renamed consistently, dates converted to datetime objects, and Year, Month and a running month_index added to each file.
  4. The 96 market-holiday rows were dropped, the last trading day of each month identified, and the monthly price return computed as the percent change between consecutive month-end closes.
  5. Year-over-year inflation was computed for headline and core CPI by merging each month against the same month twelve months earlier. Expected inflation was used as published.
  6. Each monthly series was checked for duplicate months, none of which were found, and four intermediate cleaned files were saved.
  7. The four series were merged on the month counter, restricted to January 2017 to September 2026, and the one month without CPI was dropped and logged.

No values were imputed at any stage.

If people are involved, were they aware of the data collection, and what purpose did they expect the data to be used for?¶

Two of our sources ultimately rest on people. CPI prices are collected by BLS staff from retail establishments and service providers rather than from individuals, and no personal information is involved. The Michigan series comes from a voluntary household telephone survey in which respondents agree to take part and are told that the survey concerns consumer attitudes toward the economy. Results are published only in aggregate. Respondents would reasonably have expected their answers to be used for economic research and public reporting, which is how we use them. Our dataset holds no individual-level records, so no respondent can be identified from it.

Data Limitations¶

Our sources are of high quality, but there are limitations that change how the results should be read.

Dividends are excluded. We measure price returns, so our figures understate what an investor actually earned, historically by around two percentage points a year. Every statement we make about returns means price returns. This matters most for the average level of returns. It matters less for the relationship with inflation, unless dividend yields themselves move with inflation, which they may.

October 2025 is missing, and the reason is itself a limitation. The shutdown suspended BLS collection and the month was never published. Dropping it rather than imputing keeps the data honest, but it leaves a hole created by a political decision rather than by chance. If inflation behaved unusually that month, its absence is not random.

Consecutive months are highly dependent. Any two adjacent months share eleven of the twelve months of price change that go into their year-over-year rates. Standard regression inference assumes independent observations, so ordinary p-values computed on this data would be too small and would overstate confidence. We return to this in our preregistration and do not describe any result as statistically significant without a method that accounts for it.

The sample is short and covers an unusual decade. Our window contains the COVID-19 crash and rebound of 2020 and the inflation surge of 2021 to 2023. Those episodes supply a large share of our variation, so our estimates describe this period rather than a general regularity. March 2020 alone produces the most extreme return in the dataset.

Contemporaneous matching limits what we can claim. We match inflation to returns within the same calendar month, which our mentor confirmed is appropriate for studying contemporaneous association. But a month's CPI is published partway through the following month, so investors did not know that figure while the month's returns were being generated. Our design can speak to co-movement, not to prediction or to market reaction.

Inflation is one of many forces acting on share prices. Interest rate policy, corporate earnings, employment, fiscal policy and global shocks all move markets, and several of them move alongside inflation. We do not control for any of them, so an association we observe may reflect those other forces rather than inflation itself. This is the main reason we avoid causal language.

Core CPI leaves out costs people really face. Removing food and energy produces a smoother series, but food and energy are genuine household expenses and genuine business input costs. Core inflation is a different measure, not a better one.

How these limitations could affect people¶

Claims about inflation and the stock market can influence how ordinary savers behave. A reader who treated a negative association as a reason to sell shares when inflation rises would be acting on a contemporaneous correlation estimated from 116 months of an unusual period, using returns that omit dividends, with standard errors that understate uncertainty. The risk is not hypothetical: investors who leave the market on signals like this tend to miss rebounds, and our own data show the worst month in the sample, March 2020, immediately followed by the best one. Our final report will state plainly that these results are descriptive and are not investment advice.

Exploratory Data Analysis¶

The exploratory work below has two aims: to describe each variable on its own, and to describe how pairs of variables move together. We have chosen a small number of figures that each answer a question rather than producing every chart available to us.

One constraint we have placed on ourselves is worth stating up front. Our two preregistered analyses in Part 2 are regressions of monthly return on headline and on core inflation. The fitted model is

$$\widehat{\text{core inflation}} = 0.93 + 0.66 \times \text{headline inflation}$$

Read in the usual three parts: a one percentage point increase in year-over-year headline CPI inflation corresponds to a 0.66 percentage point increase in year-over-year core CPI inflation, on average. The relationship is strong, with R² = 0.846, so headline inflation accounts for about 85% of the variation in core inflation.

The slope being below one is the substantive result. Core inflation moves in the same direction as headline but by roughly two thirds as much, which is the quantitative form of the statement that core is the smoother measure. The intercept of 0.93 says that when headline inflation is zero, core inflation is still running near 1%, which is consistent with food and energy prices falling while other prices continue to rise.

The residual plot shows no fan shape, so constant variance looks like a reasonable assumption here. The residuals are not randomly scattered either. They move in runs, with stretches of consecutive months on the same side of zero, which is the serial correlation described under Data Limitations appearing directly in a figure. It does not undermine the descriptive slope, but it is the reason we are cautious about significance testing on monthly macroeconomic data.

For our project the implication is that core adds limited independent information over this period. Our two preregistered analyses should be presented as two views of one relationship rather than as an independent replication.

The fitted model is

$$\widehat{\text{expected inflation}} = 2.35 + 0.35 \times \text{realized headline inflation}$$

A one percentage point increase in realized headline inflation corresponds to only a 0.35 percentage point increase in expected inflation, on average, with R² = 0.492. Expectations move with realized inflation, but they respond by far less than one for one.

The intercept and the 45-degree plot tell the more interesting part of the story. An intercept of 2.35 means that even with no realized inflation at all, households expect about 2.4% over the coming year, so expectations have a floor. And in 78 of 115 months, or roughly two thirds, expected inflation exceeded realized headline inflation. On the figure most points lie above the 45-degree line when realized inflation is low and below it when realized inflation is high, which is the signature of a measure that is anchored and slow to adjust rather than one tracking current prices.

For our project this means expectations are genuinely a different variable rather than a proxy for realized inflation, and their very weak correlation with returns is consistent with their being anchored, since a variable that barely moves cannot explain one that moves a great deal. This supports our mentor's advice to keep headline CPI as the primary measure and treat expectations as a descriptive comparison, and we do not preregister it.

What our exploratory analysis changed¶

The exploratory work confirmed the general direction of our research question while sharpening it in four ways.

First, the question is unchanged in direction but more modest in ambition. All three inflation measures correlate negatively with returns, as Phase I anticipated, but all three correlations are weak, at −0.159, −0.122 and −0.035. We have revised the framing so that it asks explicitly how much variation inflation leaves unexplained, rather than implying that inflation is a major driver.

Second, we now expect confounding from the pandemic to dominate. Two of our three most extreme months occurred at ordinary inflation levels. We added this to Data Limitations and will discuss March and April 2020 explicitly in the final report rather than allowing those points to drive a slope unremarked.

Third, headline and core are close to being the same variable here, with a correlation of 0.920 and an R² of 0.846. We have rewritten our second preregistration to describe itself as a related view rather than an independent test, and we will not place both measures in one model.

Fourth, expectations have been demoted from a candidate hypothesis to a descriptive comparison, on the evidence that they are anchored, with a slope of 0.35 and an intercept of 2.35, and essentially uncorrelated with returns.

Our data description needed no revision. No additional data were collected after the initial download, and the variable definitions it gives are the ones used throughout.

Part 2: Preregistration of Analyses¶

We preregister the two analyses below and commit to reporting both in our final report whatever the results turn out to be, including weak, null or opposite-direction findings.

On ordering, stated plainly. The directions of both hypotheses were fixed in Phase I, before this dataset was assembled, on the basis of the reasoning given below. Neither regression has been fitted. The exploratory section deliberately shows both relationships as scatterplots without fitted lines, and the two regressions we did run, in EDA 8 and EDA 9, take an inflation measure as the outcome rather than returns. We have, however, seen the descriptive correlations of −0.159 and −0.122, so we already expect both slopes to be negative and small. We state that openly rather than claiming an innocence we do not have.

Rules fixed in advance. Both analyses use the 116 monthly observations from January 2017 to September 2026 contained in analysis_dataset.csv. The window is set by data availability alone. We keep all genuine extreme returns, including March and April 2020. We do not impute the missing October 2025 observation. We will not alter the sample, the variables or the method after seeing the results, and any change we are later asked to make will be dated and labelled as an amendment.

Preregistration Statement 1 — Headline inflation and monthly returns¶

Hypothesis. Months with higher year-over-year headline CPI inflation have lower S&P 500 monthly price returns. We expect a negative slope.

Reasoning. Standard asset-pricing logic offers two channels. The first is a discount-rate channel: higher inflation leads the Federal Reserve to raise short-term interest rates, which raises the rate at which investors discount expected future earnings and therefore lowers the price they will pay for them today. The second is a margin channel: rising input prices compress profit margins where firms cannot pass costs on in full. Both imply a negative association. We also acknowledge a competing mechanism, since inflation often rises when demand is strong and strong demand supports earnings, which is why we treat a positive or zero slope as a fully reportable outcome rather than a failure.

Proposed analysis. Using the 116 monthly observations, with one row per calendar month, fit a simple linear regression with an intercept using the LinearRegression method practiced in HW3:

$$\text{sp500\_return\_pct}_t = \alpha + \beta_{\text{headline}} \times \text{headline\_yoy\_pct}_t$$

The outcome is the monthly S&P 500 price return in percent and the single predictor is year-over-year headline CPI inflation in percent. We will report the sample size, the fitted intercept, the fitted slope, the R², and a scatterplot with the fitted line drawn on it. We will interpret the slope as follows: a one percentage point increase in year-over-year headline inflation corresponds to a change of $\beta_{\text{headline}}$ percentage points in the monthly S&P 500 price return, on average. We will examine whether $\beta_{\text{headline}} < 0$ and will report the result whichever way it falls. Because our observations are serially dependent, we will state that ordinary inference understates uncertainty here and will not describe the slope as statistically significant on the basis of a simple regression alone.

Preregistration Statement 2 — Core inflation and monthly returns¶

Hypothesis. Months with higher year-over-year core CPI inflation also have lower S&P 500 monthly price returns. We expect a negative slope here as well.

Reasoning. Neither the discount-rate channel nor the margin channel depends on food and energy prices specifically. If the association in Statement 1 reflects those channels rather than the volatile components of the basket, it ought to survive when food and energy are excluded. If it appears only in headline inflation and vanishes in core, that would point instead toward energy prices in particular, which would itself be worth reporting.

Proposed analysis. Using the same 116 observations and the same method, fit:

$$\text{sp500\_return\_pct}_t = \alpha + \beta_{\text{core}} \times \text{core\_yoy\_pct}_t$$

We will report the same quantities as in Statement 1 and interpret $\beta_{\text{core}}$ in the same way, as percentage points of monthly return per percentage point of core inflation. We will examine whether $\beta_{\text{core}} < 0$.

Two commitments about how we will read this. First, because headline and core inflation correlate 0.920 in our sample and headline accounts for 84.6% of the variation in core, this is a related view of the same relationship rather than an independent replication, and obtaining the same sign in both is close to guaranteed rather than confirmatory. Second, because core inflation has a narrower range than headline while returns vary just as much, we expect $|\beta_{\text{core}}|$ to exceed $|\beta_{\text{headline}}|$ as an arithmetic consequence of that narrower spread. We will therefore not interpret a larger core slope as a stronger relationship, and will compare the two models on R² rather than on the size of the slope. We will not fit headline and core together in one model, since the collinearity would make both coefficients unreliable.

What we are deliberately not preregistering¶

Inflation expectations remain a descriptive comparison only. EDA 9 shows that expectations are anchored, moving just 0.35 percentage points per point of realized inflation and exceeding realized inflation in roughly two thirds of months, and EDA 2 shows they correlate only −0.035 with returns. We therefore propose no third test, and we will not convert this comparison into a hypothesis later without labelling it as an amendment.

We also commit to adding no further predictors, sample restrictions or transformations in search of a significant result. If we add a control variable in Phase III at our mentor's suggestion, it will be reported as a clearly labelled extension alongside these two preregistered models, which we will report regardless.
